# Northwind Supply style guide

Built from three published posts and the 2021 voice note. Where the posts and the note disagree, this guide follows the posts, because that's how Northwind actually writes. Anything marked **[Guess]** isn't settled by the material. Take those to the team as questions, not rules.

## Who we're writing for

The voice note says our buyers are engineers at mid-market manufacturers. The brief for this guide adds procurement managers (see flag 4) and describes both as technical, short on time, and allergic to marketing language.

## Voice

The voice note says "helpful expert." In practice, the posts show it in four ways.

- **Open on the practical point.** Each post starts with the thing the reader needs to know: a specification that "seems trivial until it isn't," a lead time that "moved from four weeks to nine," and a promise ("When we substitute a part, we tell you before it ships").
- **Make a call.** Post A doesn't list pros and cons and leave it there. It says "coarse is almost always the right answer."
- **Say what it costs us.** Post C admits "We have lost orders over this." Being straight about the trade-off is part of the voice.
- **Talk to the reader.** Mostly "we" for Northwind and "you" for the reader, though Post B never addresses the reader directly. "Customers," never "clients."

## Sentence length

Sentences run about fifteen words on average across the three posts. A few run past twenty when they're carrying a list or an explanation. Short sentences are used for emphasis, sparingly: "Every time." There are no one-line paragraphs and no bulleted lists in the posts.

## How technical to get

The voice note says to avoid jargon "where possible" but allows some, because the buyers are engineers. The posts settle it: use the trade term and don't define it. "Thread pitch," "preload," "SKUs," "mill capacity" and "buffer stock" all appear without explanation. What the posts explain is the **consequence**: a coarse thread "installs faster and tolerates a dirty hole." Assume the reader knows the word. Tell them what it means for their assembly, their install time or their order.

## Numbers

Counts, durations and percentages are spelled out: "four weeks to nine," "fourteen SKUs," "seventy percent," "thirty years," "forty units." Years are in digits ("2019").

## Terminology

## Before and after

**1. A product claim**

> *Before:* Fine threads offer superior holding power and are the premium choice for demanding applications!
>
> *After:* Fine threads hold better under vibration. If your assembly is bolted once and inspected once a year, you are paying for that in install time. Coarse is almost always the right call.

Makes a call, explains the consequence, and drops the exclamation point.

**2. A service update**

> *Before:* Due to unprecedented supply chain challenges, some customers may experience longer-than-usual lead times on select products. We apologize for any inconvenience.
>
> *After:* Stainless lead times are at nine weeks, up from four. We are holding buffer stock on the fourteen SKUs our customers order most, roughly seventy percent of our order volume. If yours is one of the others, your rep can give you a date.

Gives the number, says what we're doing about it, and tells the reader what to do.

**3. A policy**

> *Before:* Northwind reserves the right to substitute equivalent parts at its discretion to ensure timely delivery.
>
> *After:* If we need to substitute a part, we tell you before it ships. Every time. We would rather lose the order than have you find a different coating on forty installed units.

Says it as a promise, not a clause, and gives the reason.

## Flags: what this guide had to guess

1. **[Guess] Contractions.** The posts barely use them. There's one "isn't" in about 250 words, and "we are holding," "we have lost" elsewhere. The examples above are written without them. Is that a rule, or just how these three came out?
2. **[Guess] Part sizes and dimensions.** The voice note says "we spell out measurements," but none of the three posts contains a part size, thread size or dimension. For a fastener company, that's the rule that matters most. Does it mean "one-quarter inch" instead of 1/4"? What about thread designations like M8 or 1/4-20?
3. **[Guess] Length.** The posts are excerpts, so there's no evidence of how long a finished post should be.
4. **[Guess] Procurement readers.** The voice note only mentions engineers. Post B (lead times) is written as much for procurement as for engineering. Should the guide treat them as one audience or two?
5. **[Guess] Ruth.** Post A cites the founder as an authority ("Ruth has been saying this for thirty years"). The note says she writes the December letter. Can other writers quote her, and does she approve it first?
6. **[Guess] Headlines.** All three are plain and descriptive, in sentence case. Three is a small sample to call that a rule.
